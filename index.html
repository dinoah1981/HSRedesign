01 · Who we are

One high school in the South Bronx, with one measurable goal.

Comp Sci High (Urban Assembly Charter School for Computer Science) is a SUNY-authorized charter high school in Mott Haven. We opened in 2018 as New York City's first career and technical education charter school.

~480students in grades 9–12
95%Black and Latino
86%qualify for free or reduced-price lunch
22%students with disabilities
14%multilingual learners
Our footprint

One high school and, as of this fall, CSH_Alt: an alternative program for about 30 over-age, under-credited young adults (roughly 17–21). It doubles as a testing ground for parts of the larger redesign. Our 6–8 middle school opens next fall.

Who is doing the work

Mostly us. A small team of leaders (Executive Director, Managing Director of Schools, CS Department Chair) and about ten teachers is running every pilot inside a normal school year.

Who we are working with

Transcend is our design partner for the pilot year, and we are one of several high schools in a small Design Commons testing similar bets in different ways.

The mission

Every student reaches economic freedom by 25: earning at least the national median household income by that age, whether the route runs through college, a credential, or a career.

02 · Why

The target is the same. The route is different.

The all-day academic model was built when knowledge itself was scarce and valuable. Knowledge is still the foundation. But in an AI-shaped economy, what students can build, demonstrate, and judge increasingly determines their options, and a day built entirely around absorbing content leaves little room to practice any of that.

70%

of K–12 learning objectives will need to be taught differently in an AI-shaped economy.

Burning Glass Institute & aiEDU, Which Skills Matter Now? (February 2026), an analysis of AI's impact on 1,000 workforce skills and 140 high school learning objectives.
The first rung is going

Starter jobs are where AI is landing first.

Burning Glass, Harvard, and the Federal Reserve all find AI collapsing entry-level rungs. The old sequence (finish school, get a starter job, learn on the clock) is no longer reliable for our graduates.

What stays

We are keeping the baby.

Core academics stay, taught by expert teachers, because reasoning without knowledge is impossible and our students still need to read, write, and do math at the level colleges expect. The same report finds automation-exposed skills demand deeper understanding, not less.

Why now

Our students are already graduating into this.

A pilot year in 2026–27 lets us build the schoolwide model on evidence rather than on a leap of faith.

03 · The model

The day splits in two.

The morning protects the academic foundation. The afternoon is where students use it. The time we recover from compressing the core goes to real work, relationships, and reflection.

Adults work differently

A Learning Lead and a Learning Coach.

In the morning a Learning Lead pulls small groups flagged by our platforms while a Learning Coach circulates. Daily morning advisory, run as one-on-one conferences, helps students make sense of their choices and their progress.

This is V1.Next year's schedule is a waypoint toward a fully dynamic one, where classes, experiences, and intervention happen at the same time across different spaces and groups. We are not ready for that yet.
Classrooms must
  • Build strong foundational knowledge
  • Practice interpreting, evaluating, and arguing from evidence
  • Connect individual lessons to big ideas
  • Connect content to current issues in the field
Experiences must
  • Build community and connection
  • Promote agency
  • Help students explore what they enjoy
  • Build real skill, rigor, and depth
  • Make their relevance clear
04 · Competencies

Five things every graduate must be able to show.

These set the destination. Every one is a commitment for every graduate, not an aspiration for some.

1

Career Readiness

Graduates enter the world of work with skills, habits, and reference points. Not only technical skill: enough time in real or workplace-like settings that they can say, "I have done something like this before."

What it looks like
  • Communicates clearly in professional settings, orally and in writing
  • Collaborates with peers, supervisors, and clients, building a network of mentors along the way
  • Uses feedback to improve and meet clear standards
  • Shows professional habits: punctuality, preparation, communication, follow-through
  • Manages their own brand (resume, LinkedIn, mentors, relationships)
  • Develops significant knowledge, skill, and experience in at least one career field
2

Learning and Reasoning about the World

Students need both knowledge and the ability to use it. Reasoning cannot happen without facts, concepts, and disciplinary knowledge, and knowledge matters most when students can apply it.

What it looks like
  • Builds a strong foundation across math, science, history, literature, economics, civics, and technology, well enough to gain admission to and engage confidently in college
  • Draws on knowledge from different sources and classes to explain the world
  • Interprets and constructs clear, evidence-based explanations and arguments
  • Reads and accurately interprets text and other media
3

Agency, Problem Solving, and Perseverance

Students should not leave school waiting for directions. They see learning as a tool for solving problems, take initiative, and keep working when the answer is not obvious.

What it looks like
  • Takes initiative instead of waiting passively for instructions
  • Persists through difficulty, ambiguity, and setbacks
  • Breaks complex challenges into manageable steps
  • Learns new tools (including AI), resources, and even bureaucratic systems in service of real problems
  • Generates, tests, and revises solutions through iteration
4

Caring for Self and Others

A healthy life takes more than academic or economic success. Graduates leave with the habits, knowledge, and relationships to care for their physical, emotional, and financial well-being.

What it looks like
  • Recognizes, manages, and communicates emotional needs in healthy ways
  • Maintains habits that support physical health
  • Practices basic financial habits and decision-making for adult life
  • Builds and sustains respectful, supportive relationships
  • Belongs to, and contributes to, a community
  • Knows how to seek help and navigate common life systems
5

Exploring Identity and Purpose

Nobody needs a final answer to "Who am I?" by eighteen. Students do need tools, language, and habits for exploring identity, joy, purpose, and motivation over time.

What it looks like
  • Develops habits of reflection that continue beyond high school
  • Begins to articulate values, commitments, and sources of purpose
  • Explores what brings them joy: arts, music, travel, sports, hobbies, civic life
  • Explores how identity intersects with professional, economic, and social success
05 · Badges

Graduation shifts from seat time to a passport.

The afternoon is not electives bolted onto a school day. It is where a large share of our graduation requirements live. A badge is earned by completing an experience and its one deliverable. Each badge stamps a credit on the student's passport, and the passport, not a transcript of seat hours, is the record of what a graduate has actually done.

The work has to be real

Does it move a student measurably closer to a credential, a paycheck, or a real understanding of how a job or business works?

That is the test for every outside partner. If not, it doesn't make the list.

Anchor partnerships we are building
  • Skilled trades. Job shadows growing into weekly hosting in plumbing or HVAC
  • Automotive. Hands-on weekly hosting at neighborhood shops
  • Rocking the Boat. Boatbuilding, seamanship, environmental science
  • Healthcare. CPR and First Aid for whole cohorts, then a pathway to CNA, EMT, or phlebotomy
  • Small business. A real seat inside an owner-operated business: pricing, customers, payroll
  • IT. Help-desk shadowing building toward CompTIA prep
  • Swimming. A pool and a certified instructor, a trimester at a time
06 · Pilots

A pilot year, not a leap of faith.

Before the model launches, we are testing its pieces with real students and current teachers. Each pilot is built to answer an honest question: does this work, for whom, and what would it take to scale? Each one ends in a decision to scale, adapt, or sunset.

Strand 1

Compress the Core

Can students learn well with less active teacher instruction, and can a platform carry part of the load?
  • Flipped AP U.S. History~30
  • Flipped AP Computer Science A~30
  • AP Chemistry, daily independent study~8
  • AP Calculus BC, daily independent study3
  • CSH_Alt self-paced courses~30

Evidence: unit and AP results against last year, a platform audit log, observation logs, weekly student surveys.

Strand 2

Learning in the World

What design makes an experience build agency and transferable skill, and what replaces grades?
  • Engineering Design~20
  • AP Biology inquiry labs~10
  • Personal Finance module~20
  • Journalism editorial board~10
  • Book Club~12
  • Off-site swim instructionnew

Evidence: observation logs, student and cross-teacher interviews, a design-quality checklist, the Leaps experience survey.

Strand 3

Advising as Metacognition

Which conference structures, data, and tools help students make sense of their learning and choose well?
  • Conference-model advisory, two 9th-grade cohorts~60
  • Daily goal-setting advisory in CSH_Alt~30

Evidence: advisor reflection logs, conference observations, student surveys and interviews, usage of the student dashboard we built in-house.

06 · continued

How we are learning, and what we have seen so far.

With Transcend we run the pilots in rounds. Each round has a launch, a structured midpoint pause, and an end-of-round reflection, and one workbook tracks two things at once: what each pilot is teaching us, and how that is changing the core model.

Round 0Sep 9 – Oct 15Early launch for the year-long courses and advisory.
Round 1Oct 19 – Dec 18
▸ we are hereFull portfolio live. Midpoint stepback Nov 17–18.
Round 2Jan 11 – Mar 12Results feed the first experience-design workshop.
Round 3Mar 15 – May 7AP readiness, deliverable quality, advisory interviews.
Round 4May 10 – Jun 11Synthesis. What changed in the model, what's next.
Asked of every pilot, every round
  1. What are we trying to learn this round?
  2. What does strong implementation look like, concretely?
  3. What evidence will we collect?
  4. Who is benefiting, and who isn't?
  5. What is changing in the core model because of this?
Read at three levels
  • Is the design sound?
  • Is it running as designed?
  • Is it working for students?

Our own tripwire: if pilot learning has produced fewer than four real changes to the model by the end of Round 1, the system is not translating learning into change, and we pause to diagnose.

Early signals · weeks, not semesters · small samples
AP Chemistry independent study
40 hrs → 17 hrs

Roughly 40 hours of teacher correction to produce 17 hours of usable student content from AI-generated lessons: wrong visuals, prompts with no scaffolding.

The platform cannot yet be the primary instructor at AP rigor without heavy expert editing.
Flipped and independent AP · student survey, week 2
Pacing, yes.

Students value the flexible pacing, and their sense of engagement and readiness for assessments is improving. Access to teacher support, confidence solving problems alone, and knowing where they stand remain concerns.

The routines around the platform matter as much as the platform.
Afternoon experiences · student survey, baseline → week 2
+32 pts iteration

At baseline, 70%+ felt good about relationships and collaboration, but under half reported problem solving or getting started on their own. Two weeks in: iteration +32 points, agency +23, belonging +17. Adult-life skills flat at 56%.

Agency moves fast when the work is theirs. Relevance to adult life needs deliberate design.
What we cannot tell you yet.Whether students in compressed courses learn as much. The unit exam and AP comparisons against last year are the evidence that matters most, and it is too early to have them.
Where our pilots are thin.Every pilot is led by a subject-matter expert, and most test learning and agency in a classroom. Experiences that need partners, kitchens, or worksites are less tested, beyond our internship program. Swim has started and small-business partnerships are about to.
07 · The AI play

AI has a role. We are testing which one.

We do not take it as given that today's edtech AI adds value. Every tool is a pilot with a defined success metric and a scale, adapt, or sunset decision.

Where AI is load-bearing

Complexity we can't manage by hand.

Tracking evidence across dozens of badges. Flagging which students need a small group tomorrow. Putting the right information in front of an advisor and a student at the right moment. Eventually, managing a dynamic schedule.

What we are using now
  • Kira Learning. Flipped and self-paced AP content, and the CSH_Alt academic program
  • Teach to One. Adaptive algebra and pre-algebra
  • Quill. Sentence-level writing and reading intervention
  • Victory XR. VR career exposure paired with real job shadows
  • A student hub we built in-house. Powers advisory conferences
Where we are skeptical

Can a platform carry new content without a content expert in the room?

Our flipped-classroom pilots ask that plainly, along with what it costs students when it does.

What our students said

Thoughtful, limited, and not at the expense of their own thinking.

Our journalism classes debated AI regulation in schools and society. Their position was clear: AI use should be designed so it does not stunt their thinking and creativity. Much of the afternoon (engineering design, lab science, book club, journalism) is built around work AI does not do for the student.

Is AI actually better than a book?

The question we most want to work on with other schools. What evidence is anyone seeing that AI improves learning in the classroom, as opposed to handling scheduling, digesting data, and the back office?

08 · Open questions

What we are wrestling with.

We do not have these answered. They are the conversations we most want to have.

1

What will the labor economy be in seven years?

We are designing a school for a job market nobody can describe with confidence. The freshmen in our pilots will be looking for their first real jobs in it.

  • Which bets hold up across most versions of that future, and which only pay off if we guess right?
  • If the entry-level rung keeps thinning, what does a graduate need already in hand: credentials, relationships, a body of work?
  • How do we keep a school model adjustable when the target keeps moving?
2

What actual knowledge and skills let a student build "AI fluency" and use it well?

Everyone agrees students need it. Fewer can say what it is made of.

  • How much of it is the tool, and how much is the knowledge and judgment a student brings to the tool?
  • What does a student need to know to tell when the output is wrong?
  • What should we teach without AI so that students can use it effectively later?
3

How do we generate a wide variety of experiential reference points in high school?

So that when students graduate, they already have experience related to, adjacent to, or resonant with the things they will have to do in the workplace. So they can say, "I have done something like this before."

  • How wide is wide enough, and how deep does one experience have to go to count?
  • What can a school build inside its own walls, and what only a partner, a worksite, or a kitchen can provide?
  • How do we know a reference point transferred, rather than just happened?
Also on the table
  • Is AI actually better than a book?
  • What replaces grades in an afternoon experience, and how do we reliably capture growth in agency?
  • Which students thrive with independent learning, and what do the students who struggle need?
  • What does it take for a teacher to plan and lead an experience instead of a class?
  • How do we test experiences that depend on partners before we depend on them?
  • How do we get from a fixed V1 schedule to a truly dynamic one?

What are you seeing in your school? We would rather trade notes than present.

09 · Road to 2027

The model does not launch all at once.

Pilots run this year, staff build capability checkpoint by checkpoint, and the full design opens in September 2027. Dates will keep firming up as we go.

June 2026

Orient

All staff get a first look at the competencies. The pilot roster is confirmed and every pilot gets a defined success metric, so the year produces evidence, not anecdotes.

August 2026

Prepare the pilot year

A full PD week on planning for the new design and AI in the classroom. Pilot teachers get their own intensive on experience structure, pass/fail deliverables, and evidence collection.

November 2026
We are here

Pilots live, first evidence

The first pilot share-out puts early evidence and real student work in front of the whole staff, and leadership takes a preliminary read on which pilots scale.

February 2027

From pilots to design

Pilot results presented in full. The first experience designers workshop opens experience-building to every teacher, with pilot teachers co-facilitating.

April 2027

Build and choose

A protected design studio where teachers build their experiences. The catalog is published and students choose their first-trimester experiences at a family night. Year-one partner agreements signed.

June 2027

Lock the year

Final master schedule, staffing plan, and advising model. Lessons from the strongest pilots are folded into year-one experiences.

August 2027

Launch institute

Experience dry-runs, advisory launch, schedule walk-throughs. Ninth graders get an orientation and their badge passport.

September 2027

The new model is live.
